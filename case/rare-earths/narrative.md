# Case: China's 2025 rare-earth magnet export controls

## The case

On 4 April 2025, China put export licensing on seven medium and heavy rare earths, including
dysprosium- and terbium-containing magnet materials. These magnets sit in electric motors,
actuators and sensors in vehicles. Licences came slowly, magnet exports fell, and in late May and
early June 2025 vehicle makers and suppliers reported stops: Ford in Chicago, Suzuki in Japan and
several supplier plants in Europe. The hidden node here is not one firm but a country-level supply
of a material.

## The question

Two observers watch the same public signals. Observer A knows only the links that a public source
names, firm by firm, on each date. Observer B knows the full map from the start, as a multi-tier
tool or supplier compliance data could give a firm. The map value is the lead time of B minus the
lead time of A. The case also counts false alarms: how often a Chinese export control on a material
was followed by a vehicle production stop.

The rules of this case were committed before any evidence was collected
(`case/rare-earths/PREREGISTRATION.md`).

## Reading of the author

This is interpretation, not a proven finding.

1. As in the Nexperia case, the decisive early signal was a state measure, not a market signal. Here
   it was China's rare-earth regulation of 2024, which put exports under state tracing.
2. The generic dependency was public for years: government reports said that China controls most
   rare-earth separation and that magnets go into many vehicle parts. Which suppliers and parts
   depended on it became public only after the first stop. A generic statement did not help an
   outside observer to act.
3. Unlike in the Nexperia case, the trigger itself left some time to act, but only where a licence
   could come quickly. Firms responded with stockpiling; Daimler Truck said it would increase its
   inventories in the short term.
4. Most Chinese export controls on materials from 2023 to 2025 were not followed by a vehicle
   production stop. A firm that acts on every such measure would mostly act on false alarms. The
   value of a map lies in telling which measure touches its own parts.

## Limits

- **Chosen after the fact.** This case was also chosen because a disruption happened. The
  false-alarm class corrects this only for one kind of signal.
- **"No stop found" is not "no stop".** The false-alarm test rests on a logged search of public
  sources. A stop that no public source reported is not counted.
- **Several stops, one date.** Reports of the first stops came within a week; the first public
  report is used.
- **The time-to-act grid is an assumption** based on licence review times and statements about
  stocks.
- **Attention depends on the article.** "Dysprosium" reacted within days; "Rare-earth element" did
  not react until the October 2025 measures. Both articles were chosen after a probe, which the
  pre-registration says.
- **No firm-specific claim** beyond what the cited sources say. Daimler Truck is named only for its
  own public statement about inventories.
